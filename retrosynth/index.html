Retrosynth

A retrosynthesis engine. Give it a target molecule and it works backwards to compounds you can buy, using reaction templates mined from 50,016 patent reactions rather than written by hand.

Why mine the templates

The planner inside Chemical Graph Constructor runs on 102 hand-written rules at a depth of three, on a 1.5-second budget. That is the right shape for a teaching panel and the wrong one for route finding, which needs eight to fifteen steps and can afford to think. Writing the next ten thousand rules by hand is not a plan, so these are extracted automatically from atom-mapped reactions instead.

Every template is checked the moment it is made. It gets run backwards against the product of the very reaction it came from, and it has to hand back that reaction's own starting materials. 241 templates could not and were thrown away before the engine ever saw them. The surviving 97.96% is the number above, and it says the extraction works — not that the engine is good.

How good is it, honestly

On held-out reactions — ones whose templates are deliberately not in the library — the engine reaches buyable material for 26.3% of targets, and picks the same first disconnection the chemist did for 10.5%. Published systems do considerably better. That is the starting line, not the finish.

The interesting part was being wrong. A small home-made stock of 3,489 compounds looked like the obvious bottleneck, and loosening it did push the solve rate from 16% to 54% — which looked like proof. It was not. At the loosest setting, twelve of twenty-five targets were simply being declared purchasable outright; the measurement was rotting, not the engine improving. Swapping in a real 204,210-compound supplier catalogue, 58× larger with no shortcuts, moved the solve rate from 22.5% to only 26.3%. The stock was never the constraint. Every remaining failure is the search running out of budget, so the next thing to build is the part that picks which templates are worth trying.
